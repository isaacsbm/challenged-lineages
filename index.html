Redaction

Banned

Redact covers titles and creators the way a censored document is blacked out, and stamps each one with the reason. It covers every record with a challenge record. Records whose ccv:challengeOutcome is banned are stamped “Banned”, and the expurgated edition loses every other word of its title. Under Layers, “Banned or expurgated” narrows the redaction to those records alone. Hover over or select a record to lift its bars.

Challenged Lineages

Brittany Isaacson :: October 28, 2026

After Jamie Hazlitt, Alice Meets FRBR (2005)

About Challenged Lineages

This collection gathers frequently challenged or banned classic works together with the works linked to them: later books that reference, retell, adapt, or answer a classic, and older classics whose titles, plots, or forms later challenged works borrow. The focus is the link between two books, and how censorship pressure follows a set of themes, characters, or ideas from one generation of literature to the next.

In 2025 the American Library Association’s Office for Intellectual Freedom (OIF) documented attempts to censor 4,235 unique titles, the second-highest total on record. That year also had the highest share of attempts ever recorded that ended in censorship (77%). Classics remain targets. A Clockwork Orange was among the eleven most challenged books of 2025, and The Handmaid’s Tale and The Bluest Eye tied for seventh in school libraries.

Dublin Core first

Every record is a Dublin Core (dcterms:) description first. Works carry identifier, title, creator, date, type (from the DCMI Type Vocabulary), and language, and each belongs to a lineage cluster through dcterms:isPartOf. Instances, meaning editions or adaptations challenged separately, add dcterms:isVersionOf. Each challenge record is a dcmitype:Event with its date, place (dcterms:spatial), and source.

Links between works use the Dublin Core relation that fits:

  • An explicit reference, or a title or epigraph allusion, uses dcterms:references.
  • An adaptation, retelling, or parody uses dcterms:source. Instances use dcterms:isVersionOf.
  • Everything else uses dcterms:relation.

Dublin Core can say that two works are related. It cannot say that the relation matters for censorship, how strong the evidence for it is, or anything about the challenge itself.

The Challenged Collections Vocabulary

The ccv: terms are coined for this assignment. They are published at https://isaacsbm.github.io/challenged-lineages/terms#, so each term’s URI opens its definition on the vocabulary page. They are added only where Dublin Core falls short.

ccv:challengeLineage
Links a challenged work to an earlier work it derives from or responds to, where the later work’s challenge concerns content, themes, or form carried over from the earlier one.
ccv:influenceEvidence
The basis for a link, from explicit reference down to genre lineage.
ccv:challengeReason
The reason the challenger stated, recorded as stated, not as a finding.
ccv:challengeInitiator
The category of person or body that filed the challenge.
ccv:challengeVenue
Where the challenge or suppression happened: a library, a classroom, or a censor, court, customs office, or publisher.
ccv:challengeOutcome
The result, from retained to banned. “None recorded” lets an unchallenged work such as James sit in the collection without implying a ban.
ccv:challengeSource
The list or rating site a challenged title was drawn from.

Switch Edge labels between “ccv evidence” and “Dublin Core” to see what the coined terms add. Every edge has a Dublin Core relation, but only the ccv label says why the link exists and how far to trust it.

Vocabulary, profile, and questions

  • The vocabulary page publishes the seven ccv terms and their value lists as RDF. Each value is a SKOS concept with its own URI, such as ccv:outcome-banned.
  • The application profile states how Dublin Core and ccv combine in each kind of record. It is written as a DCMI Tabular Application Profile and compiled to SHACL shapes, so the data can be checked with standard tools.
  • The competency questions page asks eight questions of the data with SPARQL, once with Dublin Core alone and once with ccv. It shows where Dublin Core is enough and where the coined terms are needed.

The gap

Existing namespaces describe each book in detail, and some state that one work derives from another. None can express that both works in a relationship have been challenged, that the later work was challenged for content it inherited, or the details of a challenge: who initiated it, why, where, and with what result. OIF and PEN America document individual titles, but not as a reusable metadata vocabulary, and they do not link challenged titles to one another.

About the data

The collection has 57 works in six lineage clusters, 3 instances, 34 linked source works, and 151 challenge records. Of those, 96 come from the project’s collection inventory. Ranked-list entries, such as ALA’s decade lists, are challenge records that carry only their source and the list’s period.

The inventory states no challenge reasons, so reasons come from ALA’s yearly Top 10 Most Challenged Books lists for 2001–2024. That adds 55 challenge records and gives reasons to the two existing Twilight records. Each record keeps ALA’s exact wording in its description, and its ccv:challengeReason values point to concepts in the reason scheme. Where ALA’s wording changed over the years, such as “homosexuality” and later “LGBTQIA+ content”, one value keeps the older term as an alternative label. Narrower values are filed under the doc’s starter reasons. ALA’s lists don’t say who challenged a book or where, so ccv:challengeInitiator and ccv:challengeSource remain mostly empty and show as “not recorded”.

Creators, works, and places are linked to Library of Congress authority records, and through them to VIAF and Wikidata. Every link was reviewed before it was added. All 74 creators are linked, as are 67 of the 94 works and editions and 12 of the 14 places. LC has no record for the rest, often because it records a recent novel only through its translations. The collection’s subjects are the LCSH headings Prohibited books and Censorship.

Design

The typographic layout follows Jamie Hazlitt’s Alice Meets FRBR (2005). That poster maps the works, expressions, and editions of Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland as type joined by labelled red dotted lines. Here the same visual grammar shows challenge lineages instead of bibliographic families.

The Redact layer borrows from a second kind of document: the declassified government file, where what readers may not see is blacked out. It covers every challenged record, and the stamps keep the distinction OIF draws between books challenged, censored, and banned: a challenge is not the same as a ban. Under Layers, the redaction can be narrowed to the records this collection documents as banned or expurgated.
